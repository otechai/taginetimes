The Two-Dirham Geopolitics of Courgettes vs. The Infallible Barcodes of Inditex

23 September 2026·8 mins

At 10:45 AM inside the Marché Municipal of Bourgogne in Casablanca, a woman holding the key fob of a German crossover engages in high-stakes bilateral negotiations over three courgettes.

The vendor, standing behind a wooden crate reinforced with baling wire, has quoted seven dirhams per kilo. The buyer counters with five. To bridge this two-dirham chasm, she mobilizes an impressive analytical framework: the rainfall deficit in the Souss-Massa basin, the general unworthiness of the courgettes’ skin tone, personal acquaintance with the vendor’s maternal uncle, and a final, devastating geopolitical pivot: “I can get them for 4.50 at the Marché Bab Marrakech, wa baz.” The engagement lasts eight minutes, occupies twelve square meters of public pavement, and concludes with an uneasy truce: six dirhams total, plus an extracted handful of coriander (rabta d-qezbour) thrown into the black plastic bag free of charge. Victory is complete. Domestic honor is restored.

Ninety minutes later, the identical consumer stands inside a climate-controlled flagship store on Boulevard Al Massira Al Khadra. She selects a beige, acrylic-blend knit overshirt manufactured in Izmir, notices a loose thread hanging from the left hem, and inspects the price tag: 699 MAD.

Through the arithmetic of customs duties, international franchise licensing fees, and prime retail square-meter rentals, this item carries an unhedged 36 percent markup over its retail price in Madrid or Seville. She does not demand to see the manager. She does not cross-examine the sales associate regarding the stitching. She does not demand a complimentary pair of socks (rabta d-tqacher) to offset the transaction. Instead, she approaches the register with muted reverence, silences her phone, and extends her contactless card toward the payment terminal with the solemn hand motion of a parishioner receiving communion.

The two-dirham margin at the vegetable stall was an existential battle for human dignity. The 250-dirham markup on synthetic petroleum-derived fiber was an administrative invoice settled without appeal.

The Scapegoat at the End of the Intermediation Chain #

The institutional data from the Haut-Commissariat au Plan (HCP) and the Conseil de la Concurrence regarding food supply chains has long documented our national distribution dysfunction. Between the agricultural producer in Chtouka Aït Baha and the urban dining table, a single tomato passes through up to six intermediaries (chennaqa), changing hands inside wholesale markets (Marchés de Gros) still regulated by municipal decrees drafted when the French franc was legal tender. The intermediary class captures upwards of 60 percent of the final price, taking margins on produce that rests on wooden boards while sitting on plastic chairs drinking mint tea.

The Moroccan consumer knows this structural betrayal exists. Yet, unable to serve a subpoena to an unlicensed vegetable broker operating out of a wholesale depot at 3:00 AM, the middle-class shopper displaces the entire regulatory rage of the national economy onto the terminal node of the system: a man named Bouchaïb standing in plastic sandals next to a pyramid of turnips.

Bouchaïb is accessible. Bouchaïb has a face. Crucially, Bouchaïb’s prices are written with a dry-erase marker on an inverted strip of cardboard cut from an empty laundry detergent box.

In the semiotics of Moroccan commerce, a price written in blue marker on an Omo box is not an economic constant; it is an opening thesis in an ongoing philosophical seminar. It is an invitation to debate the cost of living, the state of the municipal council, the recent rainfall metrics, and the vendor’s personal greed. To accept the cardboard price without contest is seen as an act of civic negligence, a failure of personal vigilance bordering on treason.

The Holy Infallibility of the Optically Scanned Barcode #

The dynamic changes when the price is codified through an EAN-13 barcode printed on laminated cardstock.

Once a price is mediated through a digital scanner, the Moroccan middle class abandons its forensic skepticism. The barcode operates with the constitutional authority of a Dahir published in the Bulletin Officiel. Even though the Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire did not invent the optical scanner to calibrate markups on Turkish-sewn cargo pants, our urban consumer behaves as though questioning an electronic scanner would cause an immediate rupture in the space-time continuum.

To ask an Inditex cashier for a discount of four dirhams because the hem is uneven would break the middle-class social contract. The primary function of entering a European-branded retail space on Boulevard Al Massira or inside Morocco Mall is not the acquisition of durable textile goods; it is the public demonstration of one’s complete exemption from the indignity of haggling.

In the fast-fashion boutique, payment without discussion is the entry fee for perceived modernity. The customer pays full price to establish that she is the sort of person who does not need to ask how much things cost. She then spends the entire drive home stewing over whether Bouchaïb shorted her 150 grams on the mandarins.

The Macroeconomic Irony of Middle-Class Resistance #

The ultimate contradiction is not moral; it is mathematical.

Bank Al-Maghrib reports cash circulation (la monnaie fiduciaire) setting historic records year after year, surpassing 430 billion dirhams. The central bank publishes earnest policy briefs encouraging financial inclusion, digital interoperability, and the expansion of mobile wallets. Yet the average urban citizen maintains a strictly bifurcated monetary doctrine: hard cash is reserved for economic warfare on the pavement, while electronic liquidity is reserved for unconditional surrender to transnational retail servers.

When haggling with the informal sector over two dirhams, the consumer acts as an unpaid, hyper-aggressive auditor for the Ministry of Economy and Finance. She scrutinizes margins, checks tare weights on balance scales that were last calibrated during the Hassan II administration, and refuses to allow a penny of unearned surplus to accrue to the subsistence tier of the economy.

Once inside the mall, this consumer-protection zeal evaporates. The retail chain does not offer a free sprig of parsley. It bills three dirhams for the paper bag carrying its own corporate logo, turning the customer into a paying billboard for its seasonal inventory. The buyer accepts this line item without flinching, places the bag on the passenger seat, and spends the journey home explaining that while life in the city has become completely unaffordable, the vegetable vendors are the ones driving the country to ruin.
